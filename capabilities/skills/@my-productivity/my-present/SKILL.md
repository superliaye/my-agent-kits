# my-present

A presentation or announcement exists to change what one audience knows, believes, or does.
Everything below follows from that.

## Know what it's for

Before shaping anything, work out who the audience is, what they need from it, and the one thing
they should remember. Infer these from the request and the material, and ask only when you can't.
Every later choice is judged against them. Choose the level of detail, the story, and what to leave
out for this audience.

When revising or reviewing existing work, first understand what it already does well. Change what
was asked, plus what works against its purpose, and keep the rest.

## Let the story and the evidence shape each other

Sketch the story first, before researching broadly. The sketch shows which facts, examples, and
background matter, so research has a direction. Then gather them: the grounding facts, concrete
details, and context the user didn't mention. Then reshape the story around what you found.

The user rarely hands over everything that matters, and a single discovered fact can change what
deserves the spotlight, the order, or the whole angle. Treat the first story as a hypothesis.
Repeat the loop while research keeps changing the shape, and stop once it doesn't.

## Earn every bit of attention

The audience's attention is the scarcest resource. Every visible element must help them get the
point: each word, label, color, and line. If something only decorates, or narrates, or repeats
what the speaker will say, it costs more than it gives. Put it in the speaker's material instead,
or cut it.

Emphasis is a claim about importance, so give it only to what deserves it. Highlighting one item
among peers invites the question of why the others weren't highlighted. What you show first frames
everything after it.

## Say only what's true now

One overstated claim costs more trust than many accurate ones earn. Check each fact, status,
number, and term against its primary source as it stands today, not against a summary, a plan, or
memory. When the evidence supports only a weaker statement, make the weaker statement. When the
user's framing and the evidence disagree, say so and let them choose.

Earlier briefs, drafts, and decks are evidence to verify, not a design to inherit.

## Make meaning self-evident

The reader should never have to decode anything. A diagram carries one idea. Elements that look
alike should mean the same thing, and elements that differ should differ for a reason. When the
meaning of a shape, line, or grouping isn't obvious, say it right there, briefly, in the reader's
words, or redraw it so it becomes obvious. Name things the way this audience knows them.

Structure carries meaning too. Order, grouping, and sequence should match how the story will be
told. When the same thing appears across slides, keep it recognizable, so the reader sees it change
instead of meeting it again from scratch.

## Own the craft

Give each piece a form that fits its subject and audience, not a template. Choose format, visual
language, and motion so they serve the point. When the user asks for a brand or a style, apply it
lightly and keep what already works.

Judge the work the way the audience will meet it. Render it and look at it as a reader would before
calling it done, and say plainly what you haven't checked.

## Work with the user

Before building, propose the reshaped story, including what research changed and why. Point out
tensions early. When feedback comes in,
change what was asked and keep what the user liked. When the user brings their own words, such as a
script or a dictated draft, keep their voice and intent, and fix only what's wrong.

Deliver what the occasion needs alongside the artifact. That may include what to say, what to have
ready if asked, and where each claim comes from. Keep all of it consistent with every change.
